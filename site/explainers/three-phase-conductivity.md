## In plain terms

Take three materials that conduct electricity differently — say a poor, a
middling and a good conductor — and mix them, in fixed proportions, into a
fine-grained composite. The mixing geometry is entirely up to you: layers
inside layers, coated disks, checkerboards, anything. Every geometry gives
the blend some overall conductivity. The question: what is the *exact* best
(and worst) overall conductivity achievable, and which microstructures
achieve it?

For two materials this was answered decades ago: the celebrated
Hashin–Shtrikman bounds are exactly achievable, and the geometries that do it
are known. For three materials the same bounds are *not* always achievable —
sometimes no geometry whatsoever reaches them — and the true attainable
frontier is only partly mapped. That partly-mapped middle regime is the open
problem.

## What is known

The classical bounds (series/parallel, Hashin–Shtrikman) are proved for any
number of phases. For three phases in the plane, Nesi proved stricter bounds
when the worst conductor is scarce; Cherkaev tightened them further and found
families of layered-inside-layered structures achieving them in parts of
parameter space; wheel-like assemblages of Cherkaev and Zhang cover other
parts. In two dimensions an exact duality (Keller–Dykhne) links every
structure to a mirror problem with inverted conductivities, pinning some
special cases completely. Between the regimes where bounds and structures
meet lies a strip where the best known bound and the best known geometry
disagree — nobody knows which side is telling the truth.

## Why it is hard

The space of geometries is infinite-dimensional and the optimum is genuinely
geometric: improving a bound means finding an inequality satisfied by *all*
microstructures, while improving a structure means an explicit construction,
and the two hunts share almost no tools. The two-phase problem collapsed
because one family (laminates of laminates) turned out to be universal; for
three phases the known optimal structures are already stranger — wheels,
coated wheels — which is evidence that the honest answer may need geometry
nobody has written down yet.

## What a breakthrough would mean

This is the mathematics of metamaterials: what effective behavior can pure
geometry extract from given ingredients? The same G-closure machinery
underpins bounds for polycrystals, elasticity, and multi-material printed
structures, and conduction is its cleanest unsolved case. A closed
three-phase frontier would be the first genuinely multi-phase chapter of a
theory that has been effectively two-phase for sixty years — and every
concrete optimal structure found on the way is a design blueprint, not just a
theorem.

**In this lab it carries a medium budget and the best exact-arithmetic fit
since the Mahler census.** Laminate algebra is exactly rational: every
effective tensor the lab computes is certified in ℚ, checked twice by
independent algebraic routes plus a duality identity. The plan of record is a
census: map where bounded-complexity laminates reach the known bounds across
the parameter simplex, and isolate exact candidate structures where they do.
Nothing has been tried here yet.
