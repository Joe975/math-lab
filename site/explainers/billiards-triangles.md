## In plain terms

Take a triangle, treat it as a frictionless billiard table with no pockets, and
send a ball off in some direction. It bounces off the walls forever, angle in
equals angle out. A *periodic* path is one that eventually returns to its
starting point travelling in its starting direction, and so repeats the same
circuit for ever after.

Does every triangle have one? For a triangle with all angles under 90° the
answer has been known since 1775: join the three points where the altitudes
meet the opposite sides, and that inscribed triangle is itself a bouncing path.
For a very flat triangle — one thin sliver with a nearly straight angle — nobody
could construct a single such path, and for a long time nobody could prove one
existed.

It sounds like a puzzle. It is on a short list of the most stubborn problems in
dynamical systems.

## What is known

Right triangles were settled in the early 1990s. Triangles whose angles are all
rational multiples of a full turn were settled earlier still, and for a strong
reason: rational angles mean the ball only ever travels in finitely many
directions, which collapses the problem onto a surface where periodic paths are
plentiful. Irrational angles destroy that structure completely.

The obtuse case was pushed forward by computer. Richard Schwartz, with a
program called McBilliards, proved in 2009 that every triangle whose largest
angle is at most 100° has a periodic path. In 2018 Garber, Marinov, Moore and
Tokarsky extended the same style of argument to 112.3°, and reported that the
method runs out of road at about 112.5°.

The mechanism behind those results is worth stating, because it is what makes
the problem computable at all. Instead of reflecting the ball off a wall,
reflect the *table* and let the ball travel straight. A candidate path is then
just a straight line through a chain of mirrored copies of the triangle, and
the sequence of walls it crosses — a word — either works or does not. Crucially,
if a word works for one triangle it works for all nearby triangles too, so each
word certifies a whole patch of the space of triangles. The conjecture becomes
a tiling problem: cover the space of triangles with patches.

In June 2026 Giovanni Forni posted a preprint claiming the whole thing — that
the billiard flow in *any* polygon has a periodic orbit. It has not been through
peer review, and it is a proof by contradiction: it produces no actual path for
any actual triangle. If it holds up, the existence question closes and the
constructive question stays wide open.

## Why it is hard

Everything easy about the problem comes from angles being rational, and the
open case is exactly where they are not. The reflections generated by an
irrational-angled triangle form an infinite group, so the unfolded picture never
closes up, and there is no surface to hide behind.

The computer-assisted approach hits a different wall. As the obtuse angle grows,
the patch certified by each word shrinks and the words needed get longer, so the
search cost climbs while the ground covered falls. The two computer proofs to
date read as a race between those quantities, and past roughly 112.5° the race
is lost. Schwartz's deep searches also found parameter points around which
infinitely many patches accumulate without ever covering a neighbourhood, which
means no finite word list can finish the job by itself.

## What a breakthrough would mean

Nothing practical, and the honest answer is that this problem is studied because
it is a clean statement about an unclean object: the long-run behaviour of a
system that is neither chaotic nor integrable. Polygonal billiards sit exactly
on that boundary, and the tools for the boundary are thin. Anything strong
enough to settle triangles constructively would be a tool for that whole
regime — which is why the problem earned a place on Katok's list of most
resistant problems in dynamics rather than a place in a puzzle book.

**In this lab it is a new attack surface**, added because the unfolding
framework turns a dynamics question into exact arithmetic over the angles: a
word either certifies a region of triangles or it does not, and the answer is a
rational computation with no floating point anywhere. That is the kind of claim
this lab can check. Nothing has been tried here yet.
